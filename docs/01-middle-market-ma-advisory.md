# 01 — How Middle-Market M&A Advisory Works (US Healthcare Services Coverage)

> Context file for the expert-agent team. Last researched: 2026-11-26.
> Scope: a US middle-market investment bank's healthcare services coverage group advising on sell-side and buy-side M&A.

## 1. What "middle market" means

There is no single definition. Common working bands:

| Band | Enterprise value (EV) | EBITDA | Typical buyers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lower middle market | $10M–$100M | $1M–$10M | Lower-MM PE, independent sponsors, family offices, platform add-ons |
| Core middle market | $100M–$500M | $10M–$50M | MM PE funds ($500M–$5B funds), strategic acquirers |
| Upper middle market | $500M–$1B+ | $50M–$100M+ | Large-cap PE, public strategics, take-privates |

In healthcare services, a large share of deal count is **sub-$100M add-ons** to PE-backed platforms (physician practices, dental, behavioral, home care, physical therapy). Bankers usually earn their fees on **platform sales** (a sponsor exiting a scaled platform) and **founder/physician-owned recapitalizations**.

## 2. The coverage model

A healthcare services coverage team is organized around **relationships** (founders, physician groups, PE sponsors, strategics) and **subsectors**:

- Physician practice management (PPM) / specialty platforms (derm, ophthalmology, GI, ortho, cardiology, urology, anesthesia, women's health)
- Behavioral health (ABA/autism, outpatient mental health, substance use disorder, eating disorders)
- Post-acute: home health, hospice, personal care / home & community based services (HCBS), skilled nursing, senior living
- Ambulatory: ASCs, imaging, urgent care, physical therapy, dialysis, labs
- Dental service organizations (DSOs), veterinary (often covered by the same group)
- Tech-enabled services: revenue cycle management (RCM), value-based care enablers, pharmacy services, staffing, outsourced clinical services

The group's job is (a) to stay in front of owners years before they sell ("dialogue" coverage), (b) to know every sponsor's portfolio and appetite, and (c) to convert that knowledge into **mandates** (signed engagement letters).

## 3. Roles: analyst to MD

| Role | Typical tenure | Core responsibilities | What they own on a deal |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Analyst** | 2–3 yrs out of undergrad | Financial modeling, comps/precedents, data room setup, CIM/teaser drafting, buyer lists, market research, page-turning | The model, the comps, the "numbers on the page" being right |
| **Associate** | 3 yrs (post-MBA or promoted analyst) | Reviews analyst work, manages the deck/CIM storyline, first line of client contact on diligence, buyer-call logs | Quality control; process tracker; diligence Q&A |
| **Vice President (VP)** | 3–4 yrs | Runs day-to-day execution, manages client and buyer communications, negotiates early terms, staffs teams | The process itself — calendar, workstreams, issues list |
| **Director / Principal** | 2–4 yrs | Bridges execution and origination, leads smaller deals, develops coverage relationships | Mid-size mandates end-to-end; pitching |
| **Managing Director (MD)** | — | Origination, client relationship, pricing strategy, final negotiation, fee negotiation, firm risk/compliance sign-off | Winning the mandate and getting the deal signed at the best value/terms |

Supporting functions: capital markets/leveraged finance desk (financing color, stapled financing), sponsor coverage (relationships with PE firms), compliance/legal, fairness-opinion committee, research/data team.

## 4. Sell-side process (broad or targeted auction)

### 4.1 Typical timeline (≈ 5–9 months from kickoff to close)

| Phase | Duration | Key activities | Key outputs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0. Pitch & engagement** | 2–8 wks | Valuation perspective, buyer universe, process recommendation, fee proposal | Pitch book; engagement letter |
| **1. Preparation** | 4–8 wks | Sell-side **Quality of Earnings (QoE)** by accounting firm; management model; normalize EBITDA (owner/physician comp, one-time items, de novo losses, run-rate of acquisitions); healthcare regulatory & reimbursement review; data room build | Teaser, CIM, financial model, buyer list (often 50–150 names), management presentation draft |
| **2. First round** | 4–6 wks | Teaser outreach, NDAs, CIM distribution, buyer Q&A, process letter | **Indications of Interest (IOIs)** — non-binding value ranges, financing sources, key assumptions |
| **3. Second round** | 6–8 wks | Shortlist 4–8 bidders; management presentations; full data room (VDR); site visits; draft purchase agreement circulated; buyers' own QoE, legal, regulatory, billing/coding audit | **Letters of Intent (LOIs)** with markup of the purchase agreement |
| **4. Exclusivity / confirmatory diligence** | 4–8 wks | Select winner; exclusivity (30–60 days); negotiate SPA/APA, R&W insurance, rollover equity, employment agreements; financing commitments | Signed definitive agreement |
| **5. Regulatory & closing** | 0–6 mo | HSR (if above threshold; 30-day initial waiting period), **state healthcare transaction notice laws** (30–180 days), Medicare/Medicaid change of ownership (CHOW), licensure, payer consents | Close; funds flow; announcement |

### 4.2 Healthcare-specific sell-side issues bankers must anticipate

- **Corporate Practice of Medicine (CPOM):** in many states non-physicians cannot own medical practices, so deals use an **MSO / friendly-PC** structure. Oregon (2025) and other states have tightened CPOM rules; structure affects who the buyer can be and how cash flows are captured.
- **State transaction review laws:** California (OHCA), Oregon (HCMO program), Washington, Indiana, Massachusetts, New York, Minnesota and others require pre-closing notice and, in some states, substantive review. These extend timelines and must be in the process calendar.
- **Federal antitrust:** FTC/DOJ scrutiny of PE "roll-ups" in physician specialties (the FTC formed a Healthcare Task Force in March 2026 per Sikich). HSR form (revised 2025) requires much more information and prep time.
- **Reimbursement exposure:** payer mix (commercial/Medicare/Medicaid/self-pay), Medicare Physician Fee Schedule conversion-factor changes, site-neutral payment proposals, Medicaid cuts under OBBBA (projected >$900B over 10 years), 340B, No Surprises Act IDR exposure (anesthesia, emergency, radiology).
- **Compliance:** Stark, Anti-Kickback Statute, billing/coding audits, False Claims Act history. Buyers will run a coding audit; a sell-side pre-audit avoids late re-trades.
- **Medicare enrollment rules:** CHOW filings; the **home health "36-month rule"** restricts changes in majority ownership within 36 months of initial enrollment or a prior change in majority ownership.
- **Provider retention:** physician comp normalization (the biggest EBITDA adjustment in PPM deals), non-competes (state-dependent), rollover equity, earn-outs.

### 4.3 Process variants

- **Broad auction:** maximizes competitive tension; more leak/confidentiality risk; best for scaled, clean assets.
- **Targeted auction:** 10–30 hand-picked buyers; the most common middle-market process.
- **Negotiated sale / preemption:** one or two parties; faster; used when a strategic buyer has obvious synergies or a sponsor preempts with a "knock-out" bid.
- **Dual-track / minority recap:** sale vs. recapitalization (or IPO, rarely in MM). Sponsors increasingly use **continuation vehicles** and minority stake sales when exit markets are weak.

## 5. Buy-side process

Buy-side mandates come mostly from (a) corporate strategics without large in-house M&A teams and (b) PE platforms executing buy-and-build.

| Step | Activities |
|---|---|
| 1. Strategy & criteria | Define thesis (geography, specialty, size, payer mix, de novo vs. acquisition), valuation guardrails, financing capacity |
| 2. Target screening | Build target universe from CMS NPI/PECOS data, state licensure lists, Census County Business Patterns, industry databases (PitchBook, Capital IQ, Definitive Healthcare) |
| 3. Outreach | Proprietary outreach to owners not in a process; monitor marketed processes |
| 4. Valuation & bid strategy | Standalone value, synergies (payer contracting uplift, back-office, purchasing, ancillaries), accretion/dilution or IRR impact, walk-away price |
| 5. Diligence coordination | QoE, legal/regulatory, coding audit, IT/cyber, HR/provider contracts, real estate |
| 6. Negotiation & financing | LOI terms, structure (asset vs. stock; MSO/PC), rollover, earn-out, financing (delayed-draw term loans for add-ons) |
| 7. Close & integration | Integration plan, payer contract re-papering, credentialing |

Buy-side fees are usually lower (retainer plus success fee, or a fixed fee) because the bank is not running an auction.

## 6. Economics of the mandate

- **Retainer:** $25K–$100K per month or a fixed upfront fee, usually credited against the success fee.
- **Success fee:** in the middle market typically **1%–3% of transaction value** for $50M–$500M deals, with a minimum fee (often $1M–$3M). Smaller deals use Lehman / "Double Lehman" (e.g., 10%/8%/6%/4%/2% on successive $1M tiers, doubled) or an incentive ratchet above a target price.
- **Fairness opinion:** separate fee; required primarily for public targets or boards seeking protection. Fairness-opinion summaries in SEC merger proxies (DEFM14A) are a great public source of how banks ran comps, precedents and DCFs (see `03-public-data-sources.md`).

## 7. Key documents (what agents should be able to draft/critique)

1. **Pitch book** — situation overview, valuation ranges (football field), buyer universe, process, credentials.
2. **Teaser** — 1–2 page anonymous summary.
3. **NDA** — including non-solicit of providers/employees.
4. **CIM** — 40–80 pages: investment highlights, services, locations, providers, payer mix, KPIs (visits, RVUs, census, ADC, length of stay, revenue per visit), financials, adjusted EBITDA bridge, growth plan (de novos, acquisitions pipeline).
5. **Management presentation** — condensed CIM, delivered live.
6. **Process letters** — instructions for IOIs and LOIs.
7. **Buyer tracker / log** — every touchpoint, IOI and LOI with value, structure, conditions.
8. **Bid comparison matrix** — headline value, cash at close vs. rollover/earn-out, financing certainty, diligence status, regulatory risk, R&W insurance, closing conditions.

## 8. What makes a great middle-market healthcare banker (for agent design)

- Knows the **adjusted EBITDA** better than the client — every add-back must survive a buyer QoE.
- Translates **reimbursement policy into valuation** (e.g., a 3% Medicare rate cut on a 60% Medicare business is a ~1.8% revenue hit, which can be a 10%+ EBITDA hit at a 15% margin).
- Maintains competitive tension through LOI; prevents **re-trades** by surfacing issues early.
- Thinks like the buyer: the PE model (leverage, IRR, add-on pipeline) determines what sponsors can pay.
